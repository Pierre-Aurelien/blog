JEPA and LeJEPA from First Principles

Why predicting representations beats predicting inputs when most of the input is noise, why that idea collapses without help, and how LeJEPA repairs it with one extra loss term. Built step by step on toys that run in this page, then carried over to DNA.

Scope A JEPA is not a language model and generates nothing; it is a way of training an encoder. This tutorial builds the idea on a two-dimensional toy, derives the two ingredients of LeJEPA (Balestriero & LeCun, 2025) with proofs where they are short and clear pointers where they are not, and ends with what changes when the data is a genome. SIGReg gets a single section here; for its full construction, read Reza Bayat's SIGReg from First Principles, to which this post is a companion. Every number quoted from a toy was produced by the code that runs in the page.

Proofs, derivations, code and longer asides are folded so the main line stays short.

§1.The problem we're solving

The questionWith no labels, what do we train an encoder against?

We have a great deal of unlabeled data and we want an encoder whose output is useful for tasks we have not seen yet. What “useful” means will be made precise in §5. For now the difficulty is more basic: with no labels, what do we train against?

Every self-supervised method answers the same way at the top level. Take two related views and of the same underlying thing — two crops of an image, a video clip and its continuation, a sentence and its translation, a stretch of DNA and the spans hidden inside it — and ask the network to say something about one given the other. The families of methods differ in where they measure the error.

In (a) the loss lives in input space, so the model is graded on every token and every pixel. In (b) and (c) it lives in embedding space, and the model is graded only on what the encoder chose to keep. Two questions organise everything that follows. Why would we want to move the loss out of input space (§2, §3)? And once it is in embedding space, what stops the encoder from cheating (§4 to §7)?

§3.JEPA: predicting the representation

The questionHow does a JEPA make its prediction in embedding space?

A joint-embedding predictive architecture has two trainable parts. An encoder maps each view to an embedding. A predictor maps the embedding of one view to a guess of the embedding of the other, given whatever side information relates the two:

The side information is what makes the predictor's job well posed. In I-JEPA (2023) the view is an image with blocks removed, is the removed blocks, and is where they were. In V-JEPA (2024) the same is done with space-time tubes of video. In a world model and are consecutive observations and is the action taken. When the views are symmetric, as two random crops are, the predictor can be dropped and the loss simply pulls embeddings together.

Because the target is itself learned, the encoder is free to leave out of it anything that cannot be predicted from . Compare with Proposition 2.1: there, noise is modelled and then averaged away; here it is never represented. The approach scales. V-JEPA 2 was pre-trained on over a million hours of video and then, with under 62 hours of robot data, turned into an action-conditioned world model that plans pick-and-place on real arms without task-specific training.

But look again at the loss. Both of its sides pass through the same encoder.

§4.Collapse

The questionWhy does a prediction loss in embedding space collapse?

Set for every input, and let the predictor output . Then exactly. The loss is perfectly minimised by an encoder that has thrown away everything. A reconstruction loss cannot be cheated this way, because a constant embedding cannot rebuild the input; that protection is precisely what JEPA traded for the right to ignore noise.

Failure comes in two grades. In complete collapse all inputs map to a single point. In dimensional collapse the embeddings spread out, but only inside a low-dimensional subspace of , and the remaining dimensions carry nothing.

From isotropic to Gaussian

Proposition 5.1 fixes only the covariance. The paper goes further in two steps, which we state without proof. With ridge regularisation, anisotropy also increases the bias of the probe for some task, so isotropy is optimal for linear probes on both counts. And for nonlinear probes:

Theorem 5.2 (LeJEPA, Theorem 1; admitted) For -nearest-neighbour and kernel probes, among embedding distributions with a fixed scalar covariance budget, the isotropic Gaussian is the unique minimiser of the integrated squared bias.

The idea of the proof is that the leading bias of a local-averaging probe is governed by a smoothness functional of the embedding density, and at fixed variance the Gaussian minimises it. It is also the maximum-entropy density at fixed variance; the two facts are cousins.

What “optimal” means here

The statement is minimax over unknown tasks, for linear, -NN and kernel probes on frozen features. It says nothing about fine-tuning, and if you know your task in advance some other geometry may serve it better. It is a principled default, which is more than the field had.

§6.SIGReg in one section

The questionHow can a loss computed on one mini-batch push embeddings toward that distribution?

CodeSIGReg in a dozen lines of PyTorch
def sigreg(z, n_dirs=256, t_max=3.0, knots=17):        # z: (N, K)
    t   = torch.linspace(0, t_max, knots, device=z.device)
    dt  = t_max / (knots - 1)
    w   = torch.full_like(t, 2 * dt); w[[0, -1]] = dt   # trapezoid, doubled for t<0
    phi = torch.exp(-t**2 / 2)                          # target CF, also the window
    a   = torch.randn(z.shape[1], n_dirs, device=z.device)
    a   = a / a.norm(dim=0)                             # fresh unit directions each call
    u   = (z @ a).unsqueeze(-1) * t                     # (N, n_dirs, knots)
    err = (u.cos().mean(0) - phi)**2 + u.sin().mean(0)**2
    return ((err * w * phi).sum(-1) * z.shape[0]).mean()

§7.LeJEPA: putting the two terms together

The questionWhat happens when the prediction loss and SIGReg are combined?

Take views of each of samples, embed them all with the same encoder, and let be the mean embedding of sample over its views. The objective is

Codeone LeJEPA training step
def lejepa_step(encoder, opt, views, lam=0.05):
    V, N = views.shape[:2]                                # views: (V, N, ...)
    z = encoder(views.flatten(0, 1)).view(V, N, -1)       # (V, N, K), one shared encoder
    center = z.mean(0, keepdim=True)                      # per-sample mean over views
    pred = (z - center).square().mean()                   # agree across views
    reg  = torch.stack([sigreg(z[v]) for v in range(V)]).mean()
    loss = (1 - lam) * pred + lam * reg
    opt.zero_grad(); loss.backward(); opt.step()
    return pred.item(), reg.item()

What the paper reports

79% linear-probe accuracy on ImageNet-1k with a ViT-H/14; stable training up to a 1.8-billion-parameter ViT-g; a training loss that tracks downstream accuracy closely enough to select models without labels. For a scientist the most interesting result is a small one. On the Galaxy10 astronomy dataset, a ConvNeXt-V2 Nano pre-trained with LeJEPA on Galaxy10 itself reaches 76.5% with a frozen backbone, against 71.4% for a DINOv3 ViT-S/16 transferred from natural images. In-domain self-supervision on a small dataset beat transfer from a frontier model. Keep that in mind for §9.

A sense of proportion: 79% is far from the best ImageNet linear probes, which belong to DINO-class models trained on much larger curated datasets. LeJEPA's claim is not the record. It is that a fifty-line objective with one hyper-parameter and a theory gets this close, on any architecture, without the usual fragility.

TakeawayLeJEPA adds one term to the prediction loss, weighted by . That weight is the price above which a direction the views disagree on is let go. SIGReg does not forbid collapse; it makes it unstable.

§8.Caveats

The questionWhat does LeJEPA not guarantee?

SIGReg prevents geometric collapse, not semantic triviality

It guarantees well-spread dimensions. It does not guarantee that they encode anything you care about. Toy 1 cuts both ways: the model keeps whatever the views share, so if two views share a nuisance variable — the camera, the sequencing batch, the GC content — that variable is perfectly predictable and will happily fill the embedding. Views must be built so that nuisances differ between them.

“Optimal” is a minimax statement about frozen-feature probes

It is a default for unknown tasks, not a theorem about your task, and it is silent about fine-tuning.

The guarantee concerns the minimum, not the path

Cramér–Wold says the only population minimiser of SIGReg is . It says nothing about saddles, and §7 showed that the collapsed point survives as one. With finite , finite and a finite quadrature, what is actually minimised is an approximation; resampling the directions at every step is what makes a small sufficient.

On discrete sequences, latent prediction alone has not been enough so far

The most careful study to date, ProteinJEPA (2026), finds that a JEPA term added to masked-language modelling helps, and that JEPA without the token loss does badly. We come back to this below.

§9.From pixels to nucleotides

The questionWhy have token-level objectives disappointed on DNA, and what could a JEPA change?

Genomic language models copy the NLP recipe: mask nucleotides and recover them (DNABERT-2, Nucleotide Transformer, GPN), or predict the next one (HyenaDNA, Evo). Token-level likelihoods are the natural tool for scoring single-nucleotide variants, and such models are genuinely useful there. As representations of regulatory function they have disappointed. Tang, Somia, Yu and Koo (Genome Biology, 2025) probed four pre-trained models on six functional-genomics tasks and found that their embeddings gave little to no advantage over one-hot sequence fed to a standard CNN, and that the CLS summary token carried barely more than dinucleotide statistics.

That is not a verdict on next-token prediction, which on text is the most successful objective the field has. It is Proposition 2.1 applied to a genome. The following is an argument, not a theorem.

The entropy budget is in the wrong place. Under a tenth of the human genome shows evidence of selective constraint. Elsewhere, the next base is close to a draw from the local composition, which no model can predict, or it sits in a repeat, where it can be copied without understanding anything. The two cheapest ways to lower the loss, -mer statistics and repeat memorisation, are both non-functional. A CLS token that encodes dinucleotide content is not a bug; it is what the objective asked for. In the language of Toy 1, neutral sequence is a distractor with enormous variance, and reconstruction follows variance.

Four letters carry no meaning alone. A subword of text is already a unit of sense. A nucleotide carries at most two bits, and function appears in combinations: degenerate motifs of 6 to 20 bases, their spacing and orientation, under the control of elements up to a megabase away. A token loss charges a silent substitution exactly as much as one that destroys a binding site. A latent loss can be indifferent to the first and sensitive to the second, if the views teach it to be.

Nothing in the loss shapes the vector people actually use. Most laboratories consume these models as frozen feature extractors with a small head on top. A masked-token loss trains per-position logits; the geometry of the pooled embedding is an accident. §5 says what that geometry should be, and LeJEPA trains it directly.

What has been tried

The idea is months old in genomics and the evidence is early. JEPA-DNA (Larey et al., February 2026) continues the pre-training of DNABERT-2, NTv3 and HyenaDNA with an added latent objective on the pooled embedding — moving-average target encoder, small predictor, span masking — while keeping the token loss and adding VICReg-style terms. The authors report mean linear-probe AUROC gains of 2.6%, 2.2% and 3.3% on the three backbones and improvements on 6 of 8 zero-shot tasks. I could not establish whether the baselines received matched continued training, so read this as encouraging, not decisive; note also that its anti-collapse machinery is the pre-LeJEPA zoo. GenoJEPA (Wang et al., April 2026) drops token reconstruction, patches the sequence as a vision transformer would, and reports strong frozen-feature results on 55 tasks; it is an unreviewed preprint whose baselines I have not examined. ProteinJEPA (Ofer, Linial, Shahaf, May 2026) is on proteins but is the one to learn from: under matched wall-clock budgets, a latent loss with SIGReg and no teacher, added to MLM at the masked positions, wins 10 tasks, loses 3 and ties 3 of 16 on a pre-trained ESM2-35M (11/2/3 on ESM2-150M); from scratch it is mixed (6/8/2); and JEPA without the token loss loses badly on every pre-trained backbone.

The honest reading: on biological sequence, latent prediction currently complements token prediction. The token loss is the right instrument at single-base resolution, the latent loss is the right instrument for the geometry of function, and a genome needs both. In my search I found no published genomic model trained with the pure LeJEPA recipe. That experiment is open.

A blueprint

Toy 1 and §7 say the model keeps what the views agree on. So the design question is not the architecture. It is: which pairs of inputs share function and differ in everything else? In decreasing order of how well established they are: masked spans at the scale of motifs, tens to hundreds of bases, since a single hidden base can be guessed from composition while a hidden enhancer segment cannot; the reverse strand, an exact symmetry of the molecule; orthologous sequence, where two enhancers tens of millions of years apart are two augmentations of one function made by nature, selection holding the function while drift scrambled the rest (untested, and it needs a way to pair loci); other modalities, predicting the embedding of an accessibility or methylation track from the sequence beneath it; and, for liquid biopsy, sets of fragments, where two disjoint random subsets of the cell-free fragments of one blood draw are two views of one patient state. That last one is speculation on my part, and it walks straight into the first caveat of §8: the germline genotype is shared exactly by both subsets, and is a perfect shortcut.

The experiment that would settle it

Generate sequences from a background with realistic -mer statistics and planted repeats. Plant a small grammar: two degenerate motifs whose relative spacing determines a binary label never shown in pre-training. Let be the fraction of positions inside motifs. Pre-train one small transformer three ways at matched compute — MLM, LeJEPA on span-masked views, and both — and fit a linear probe on the pooled embedding. The claim of this section predicts that the MLM curve falls toward chance as goes from 0.5 to 0.01 while the others hold. It is refuted if the curves coincide, or if LeJEPA's embedding turns out to encode background composition. Add an arm where the two views share a nuisance feature, to measure the shortcut. And compare against a supervised one-hot CNN, as Tang et al. did, not only against other foundation models.

TakeawayA genome looks more like an image than like a text: most positions are either unpredictable or copied. A latent objective can ignore them, but only the choice of views tells it what to keep, and on biological sequence latent losses have so far complemented token losses rather than replaced them.

Closing thought

The appeal of LeJEPA is not a benchmark number. It is that each design choice is the answer to a question you can state, and most of the answers can be checked on a two-dimensional toy in a browser. What remains genuinely open is the part no theorem covers: for a given kind of data, which two things should be called views of the same thing. For images the community has twenty years of answers. For genomes it has barely started asking.
