Modern Hopfield Networks for Multiple Instance Learning

From an energy function to attention, from attention to pooling a million instances, and what that pooling can and cannot see. With toys that run in this page, then two applications: immune repertoires and cell-free DNA.

Scope This tutorial derives the modern Hopfield network of Ramsauer et al. (2021) from its energy, shows why its update rule is transformer attention, and then studies one use of it in detail: as the pooling layer of a multiple instance learner, where a bag of up to millions of instances gets a single label and only a tiny fraction of instances carry the signal. The analysis of pooling in §6 to §8 is worked out on a Gaussian toy where everything has a closed form, and every closed form is checked against simulation. The applications are DeepRC (Widrich et al., NeurIPS 2020) and Freenome's fragment-level deep learning model for colorectal cancer (Widrich et al., Workshop on Structured Data for Health, ICML 2026). Companion to JEPA and LeJEPA from First Principles.

Proofs, derivations, code and longer asides are folded so the main line stays short.

§1.The problem: learning from bags

The questionWhat makes learning from a bag of a million instances, with one label, hard?

In ordinary supervised learning every example has a label. In multiple instance learning (MIL) the label belongs to a bag of instances, and nobody tells you which instances are responsible for it. A positive bag contains some witnesses, instances that carry the signal, mixed into a background that looks the same as in a negative bag. The fraction of witnesses is the witness rate .

Two problems from biology show how extreme this can get. An immune repertoire is the set of T-cell or B-cell receptor sequences in a blood sample: hundreds of thousands of sequences per person, of which a handful may recognise a given virus. DeepRC was built for witness rates down to 0.01%. A liquid biopsy yields millions of cell-free DNA fragments per blood draw; in early-stage cancer the tumour-derived fraction can be below one in a million. In both cases is to , is or less, and there are only a few thousand labelled bags.

The pooling step must be permutation invariant (a bag is a set), must cost (self-attention among a million instances is out of the question), and must not drown three witnesses in a million bystanders. The rest of this tutorial is about that box.

§3.Associative memory, briefly

The questionWhat does associative memory have to do with pooling?

An associative memory stores patterns and, given a partial or noisy query , returns the stored pattern it most resembles. Hopfield's 1982 network does this with binary patterns and the quadratic energy , whose minima are near the stored patterns; retrieval is descent on . Its weakness is capacity. Each stored pattern perturbs the energy around every other one, and beyond about patterns the minima merge and retrieval fails.

The fix, due to Krotov and Hopfield (2016) and Demircigil et al. (2017), is to make the energy steeper: replace the square by a faster-growing function , so that is dominated by the best-matching pattern and the others barely interfere. With the capacity grows like ; with it becomes exponential in . Ramsauer et al. (2021) took the exponential version to continuous states, and that is where attention appears.

§5.It is attention

The questionWhy is one step of that network exactly attention?

Let the state and the stored patterns come from learned projections of raw inputs: a query , keys , and let the retrieved content be read out through values . One update of the network then reads

which is transformer attention, with . Attention is one step of energy descent in a modern Hopfield network whose memory is the current context. The hopfield-layers library packages three uses of this. When query and memory both come from the data it is ordinary attention. When the memory is a learned parameter it is a trainable lookup table. And when the query is a learned parameter and the memory is the bag, it is a pooling layer:

This is a close cousin of the attention-based MIL of Ilse, Tomczak and Welling (2018), where the score of an instance is a small network's scalar output. With a linear key map the two coincide up to parametrisation. What the Hopfield view adds is an energy, a temperature with a meaning, and a theory of what is retrieved.

Codea Hopfield pooling layer in PyTorch, returning the energy too
class HopfieldPooling(nn.Module):
    def __init__(self, d_in, d_k, d_v, beta=None):
        super().__init__()
        self.q    = nn.Parameter(torch.randn(d_k) / d_k**0.5)  # the learned state pattern
        self.key  = nn.Linear(d_in, d_k)
        self.val  = nn.Linear(d_in, d_v)
        self.beta = beta if beta is not None else d_k**-0.5
    def forward(self, x):                        # x: (N, d_in), one bag
        logits = self.beta * (self.key(x) @ self.q)             # (N,)
        a = logits.softmax(0)
        z = a @ self.val(x)                                     # pooled bag vector
        energy = logits.logsumexp(0) / self.beta                # see §7, §8
        return z, a, energy

TakeawayOne update of a modern Hopfield network is attention. With a learned query and the bag as its memory it becomes a pooling layer: one query against keys, linear in the bag size, with as the knob between mean and max.

The pooled value is a convex combination of instance values. It tells you what the attended instances look like, never how many there are. For detection that is often fine. For any target that depends on burden — tumour fraction, clonal expansion, viral load — it is a structural limitation: once attention has found the witnesses, fifty of them and five hundred of them give the same .

The remedy costs nothing: the energy is computed on the way to the softmax. Concatenate it to before the output head, or use several heads at different . Codefour attention diagnostics in PyTorch
def attention_diagnostics(logits, values):
    a     = logits.softmax(0)
    n_eff = torch.exp(-(a * a.clamp_min(1e-30).log()).sum())      # exp H(a)
    spread = logits.max() - logits.logsumexp(0) + math.log(len(a))  # 0 if uniform
    z = a @ values
    g = a * (values - z).norm(dim=-1); g = g / g.sum()            # who receives gradient
    n_eff_grad = torch.exp(-(g * g.clamp_min(1e-30).log()).sum())
    return n_eff, spread, n_eff_grad

§9.Application: immune repertoires (DeepRC)

The questionHow does DeepRC apply all this to immune repertoires?

Each person carries many millions of distinct T-cell receptor sequences, produced by random recombination; a sequencing run samples a few hundred thousand. An infection expands the few clones that recognise it. The task is to predict immune status, such as cytomegalovirus seropositivity, from the repertoire alone: a bag of or more short amino-acid sequences, one label, no idea which sequences matter. Widrich et al. describe it as MIL with two orders of magnitude more instances than the field had considered, with witness rates down to 0.01%.

DeepRC is Figure 5.1 with specific choices, which I read from the reference implementation. The instance embedding is a one-dimensional CNN over the amino-acid sequence (kernel size 9, 32 kernels, SELU activations, 20 amino-acid channels plus 3 position channels), max-pooled over positions so that a motif is detected wherever it sits. The keys come from a two-layer SELU network whose scalar output is the attention logit, which is a learned fixed query absorbed into the last linear layer. The values are the sequence embeddings, and the head is a small output network on .

An engineering idea, and its blind spot

Holding activations for sequences per bag is too expensive, so the forward pass is run without gradients in mini-batches of sequences to get attention logits, the top 10% of sequences are kept, and only those are re-embedded with gradients. It is a hard pre-filter by the model's own attention, affordable precisely because the pooling is a single query. In the light of Proposition 8.1 it carries a risk worth knowing: a witness outside the current top 10% receives no gradient at all, so early attention has to be diffuse enough for witnesses to make the cut by chance.

The paper evaluates on four families of data: simulated repertoires, LSTM-generated repertoires with implanted motifs at witness rates from 10% down to 0.05%, real repertoires with implanted motifs at 1% and 0.1%, and the real CMV cohort of Emerson et al. (2017). DeepRC is reported to outperform the compared methods across them, and the attention weights recover the implanted motifs, which is the interpretability argument for this architecture: the model points at its witnesses.

Where the toy analysis bites

implanted-motif witnesses are strong (a motif is present or absent, so is large once the CNN has a filter for it), which is the first regime of Figure 7.1, where sharp attention and even max pooling do well. Natural disease-associated sequences are degenerate and partially public between individuals, closer to the second regime, where counting matters. A strong classical baseline on CMV, from Emerson et al., is tellingly a burden test: count how many CMV-associated sequences a repertoire contains. That is a multiplicity statistic, of the kind Proposition 8.2 says the pooled value cannot represent.

TakeawayDeepRC is Hopfield pooling with a convolutional instance embedding and a top-10% attention filter for memory. Its interpretability comes from attention pointing at the witnesses; its strongest competitor on real data counts them.

§10.Application: cell-free DNA (Freenome's FLDL)

The questionHow does FLDL apply it to cell-free DNA, and what does the paper show?

Source note This section describes Learning Extremely Sparse Signals in High-Dimensional Cell-Free DNA Data Using Modern Hopfield Attention for Colorectal Cancer Detection (Widrich, Patel, Rumetshofer, Ulz, Coil, Royce, Lin, Bourgon, Dutta; Workshop on Structured Data for Health, ICML 2026, OpenReview). It was revised on October 2, 2026 against the paper itself; the first version relied on a third-party summary and overstated two results, corrected below. The first author is also the first author of DeepRC. A detailed audit of the system is in Auditing a Fragment-Level Deep Learning System.

Plasma contains millions of short DNA fragments shed by dying cells. In a patient with an early colorectal tumour or an advanced precancerous lesion, a minute fraction comes from the lesion and carries its methylation pattern. The paper cites witness rates as low as 0.0001% in low-shedding cases, among 1 to 10 million fragments per sample. Classical pipelines first reduce each sample to hand-built features, such as per-region methylation rates, and then classify. Fragment-level deep learning (FLDL) keeps the fragments as the instances and trains end to end.

In the paper, each fragment is embedded from five inputs, each through its own encoder: its sequence with per-CpG methylation state (one-hot, through a 1-D CNN), four methylation statistics, genomic position, strand, and a pre-computed 128-dimensional HyenaDNA embedding. learned prototype queries pool the bag, an MLP head predicts cancer status alongside auxiliary tasks, and 25 models from five repeats of 5-fold cross-validation are averaged. Training used 4,394 samples: 377 cancers, 548 advanced precancerous lesions and 3,469 controls. The independent clinical test set has 930 samples (211 cancers, 388 lesions, 331 controls), and a contrived set has 148 replicates of one advanced-cancer sample diluted into pooled healthy plasma just above the baseline's detection limit.

At 90% specificity, with each threshold set on the 331 test controls, the best configuration (PD-FLDL, whose input regions are pre-filtered using 825 additional healthy individuals) detects 189 of 211 cancers (89.6%) and 117 of 388 lesions (30.2%), against 186 (88.2%) and 105 (27.1%) for the methylation baseline. On the contrived set its figure shows 0.82, that is 122 of 148 replicates, against 104 (70.2%); the text's 84.2% is not a possible count out of 148. The learning curve, measured with five-model ensembles on the contrived set, had not flattened.

Two comparisons speak directly to this tutorial. Replacing Hopfield pooling by max pooling (for each embedding dimension, the fragment that maximises it) cost 10 to 13 points on lesions and cancers alike and dropped the contrived call rate to 6–16%. Widening the input to the full capture panel (ER-FLDL), with about twice as many fragments per bag as the CRC panel, stayed level with the baseline on the clinical set (110 lesions, 186 cancers) but fell below it on the contrived set (99 replicates against 104). An earlier version of this section, written from a summary, said that widening the panel “did not degrade” the model; the paper does not support that, and every one of these differences between FLDL variants and the baseline is within the noise of a single test set of this size.

The max-pooling result is what §7 predicts. Early-lesion fragments are many-and-weak — partially methylated, overlapping with normal tissue variation — which is the regime where max pooling loses to moderate . The panel result is subtler. Uninformative fragments with low scores receive weight that is exponentially small, and by Proposition 6.1 adding them changes , which a modest increase in compensates. But the compensation must be learned from the same few thousand labels, and the fragments a wider panel adds are not all low-scoring: regions with sporadic background methylation in healthy people put fragments in exactly the tail that attention selects. The paper's best variant is the one whose input was filtered to remove such regions, with bags about four times smaller than the unfiltered CRC panel's.

Three questions to put to any model of this family

First, the gains over a strong baseline are 12 of 388 lesions and 3 of 211 cancers, and each threshold is estimated from the same 331 controls, so the full standard errors (about 4.0 and 2.7 points) are wider than binomial intervals suggest; is the paired difference resolved, and are the splits free of site and batch structure that an end-to-end model could exploit? Second, lesion burden and tumour fraction are multiplicity variables; is the log-partition output of the pooling layer used, or only ? Third, what is on real bags, and does it differ between true positives and false negatives? A missed cancer with diffuse attention is an embedding problem (small ); one with saturated attention on a background outlier is a pooling problem.

TakeawayFLDL scales the same skeleton to millions of fragments. Its clearest result is that max pooling fails; its finer comparisons are a few patients wide and within the noise of a single test set.

Closing thought

The modern Hopfield network is usually presented as an explanation of attention. For set-level problems in biology it is more useful read the other way round: as a statistical instrument with a known operating characteristic. One learned query against a million keys is a tilted average; its temperature trades the fraction of attention on witnesses against the number of instances heard; its log-partition is the likelihood-ratio statistic for sparse mixtures; and its pooled value cannot count. Each of those statements fits in one line, and each was checked here on two thousand Gaussian numbers before being trusted on a million DNA fragments.
